A Lawyer's Photo Should Not Look Like a Designer's

Almost all headshot advice resolves to the same four words: smile, good lighting, neutral background. Correct, and not very useful — because the thing a client is looking for differs completely between a family lawyer and an architect, and a photo that works for one is actively wrong for the other.

What each profession is really signalling

Nobody hiring a solicitor wants to see approachability first. They want to see that this person has done this before and will not be rattled. That reads as restraint: sober suiting, minimal expression, nothing competing for attention in the frame. The lawyer notes lean deliberately traditional, which is not conservatism for its own sake — it is matching what a client scans for in about two seconds.

Put the same photo next to an architect's and it looks like the architect is hiding something. Creative professions are the one category where visible personality is the credential, and a corporate-restraint portrait reads as bland rather than reliable.

Medicine: the white coat question

Clinical professions come with a genuine tension that other fields do not have. Patients want competence, but they also want to be treated kindly, and those pull the image in opposite directions.

Practically, the answer usually depends on who is looking. Hospital directories and professional profiles skew towards clinical neutrality — see the notes for doctors and the related nurse headshots guidance, which splits along exactly this line. Practices aimed at patients directly can afford considerably more warmth, and dentists in particular suffer from the opposite problem — people arrive already nervous, and a severe practice-site photo makes it worse.

Where people overshoot

Two categories reliably go too far in opposite directions.

  • Property. The sales instinct pushes towards the broadest possible grin, and it backfires — heavy retouching and a huge smile read as "someone is about to sell me something". The realtor guidance keeps it much calmer than agents expect.
  • Finance. Not hyper-polished either. Someone handing over their accounts is looking for steadiness, not charisma — see accountant headshots. Highly retouched is worse than slightly plain here.
  • Therapy. The one case where warmth wins outright. Nobody books a first session with someone who looks severe, and therapist photos are accordingly the softest of any of these.

The two things that should not change

Whatever register you land on, two things apply everywhere:

  1. Background discipline. Busy backgrounds damage credibility more than almost anything else. One plain background beats a plausible-looking office every time.
  2. Consistent crop. Eye line at roughly the same height and the same amount of shoulder across every team member's photo. A grid where heads sit at different sizes reads as careless regardless of how good the individual images are.
