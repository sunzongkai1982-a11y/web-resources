Two Warm-Weather Hazards That Get Underestimated

Contact a vet or an animal poison helpline first. Neither of these improves with observation at home, and both have treatments that work better the earlier they start.

Warm weather produces two recurring patterns, and in both cases owners are making the same error: judging severity by the size of the event rather than by where it landed or what it contained.

Stings: count the site, not the number

The instinct is to count stings. There were three, that seems minor. But animals do not retreat from insects — they go at them, particularly with anything flying — which puts stings on the muzzle, inside the mouth, and on the paws. Swelling in the mouth and throat has consequences quite different from three stings on a flank, and it can develop fast enough that "monitoring overnight" is not available as an option.

The second reason counting misleads: reaction is not linear. Sensitisation means a subsequent sting can produce a response far out of proportion to previous ones, and none of that is visible from the outside. Facial swelling, difficulty breathing, sudden lethargy or collapse — these are not wait-and-see presentations. What to watch for is set out in the notes on bee and wasp stings.

Ponds: quantity is not reassuring

Standing water with a green film on it — a green-blue scum on still water, most often in late summer — can contain cyanobacteria. The thing people get wrong is assuming the exposure needs to be substantial. Swimming through it, drinking a few mouthfuls while fetching, or grooming it off the coat afterwards is enough. There is no quorum.

A second error is assuming it looks obviously toxic. Water can look perfectly normal and still carry it, so "it seemed clean" is not evidence of much. The exposure mechanism and what follows are covered in the notes on blue-green algae poisoning.

Only one of them went in the water

A question that comes up regularly: several animals were exercised together and only one entered the suspect water. The others did not go in, they seem fine — leave it? The reasoning for why that is not sound, and what to do for each animal, is worked through in the answer to only one dog swam in it, and the short version is that "was present but unobserved" is worth taking seriously rather than assuming.

The pattern worth extracting

Both of these fool people for the same reason: the event looks small. A handful of stings and a drink from a pond both read as ordinary, and both are mechanisms where small inputs produce disproportionate results. When you call, describe what went in or what stung, where it landed, roughly when, and your animal's weight — everything you need to structure that is available at PetTriage.
