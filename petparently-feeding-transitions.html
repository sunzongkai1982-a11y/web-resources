Feeding Charts Do Not Fail at the Portion

When a captive animal starts gaining or losing at the wrong rate, the first suspect is nearly always the portion. Sometimes that is right. More often the portion is fine and the schedule has quietly become the wrong one, because the animal changed stage and the routine did not change with it.

The schedule has an expiry date

Juveniles and adults of the same species are, feedingly speaking, almost different animals. A growing one needs more frequent meals and a different prey size; an adult on that same routine is simply being overfed, and the weight arrives slowly enough that it reads as normal until it does not. The reverse happens too — an adult schedule applied to an animal that is still growing.

The point is not to memorise a table. It is to notice that the table has a column for age, and that the age column moves. The feeding guides are organised around that, and the life-stage pages exist because the transitions are where the mistakes are made rather than at either end.

Weigh, do not eyeball

Body condition is the only feedback loop that catches a schedule error early, and it requires a scale rather than a judgement. A monthly weight on the same scale, written down, turns "looks fine" into a trend line — and a trend line is the thing that tells you a transition was missed three months ago. Portions get adjusted from that line, not from a chart alone.

Snakes make the clearest example, because the interval between meals is long enough that a wrong one goes unnoticed for months. The ball python page is worth reading with that in mind, and the feeding tool is there for the cases where the interval rather than the amount is the question.

The other failure: changing too fast

The opposite error is correcting all at once. Food changes, like most things in husbandry, go better stepped — a new item introduced alongside the old, rather than switched outright. An animal that refuses the new thing entirely is usually telling you the change was too abrupt, not that the food is wrong.

What to watch

Three things, monthly: weight, whether the last meal was actually taken, and whether the schedule still matches the animal's stage. Those three catch nearly everything, and none of them require a portion chart.
