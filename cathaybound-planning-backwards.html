Planning Backwards: Why Wish-List Itineraries Fall Apart

The almost universal first-draft method is to write down everything worth seeing, then work out how to connect it. This inverts the only constraint that actually bites, and the cost shows up as two days spent in transit that were never budgeted.

Start from distance, not desire

Trip planning has one dominant variable — how far things are from each other — and it is also the one people consult last. Write the list, then place a map over it, and half the list usually reveals itself as belonging to a different trip.

Concretely: pick the region first, then the cities inside it, then what to see in each. Working outward from geography produces routes that make sense; working inward from a wish list produces routes that make sense on paper. The plans under day-by-day itineraries are sequenced this way, around clusters rather than around a list.

Two things per day is not timidity

First-time visitors routinely schedule four or five. Two is realistic, and three is tolerable only when two of them are near each other.

The reason is not stamina. It is that every item has a getting-to and a being-there, and the getting-to in a Chinese city is larger than it appears — a site on the far side of town is forty minutes of metro even though it is on the same line. Two substantial things plus the walking around them generally fills a day more completely than it sounds.

Underscheduling has a better return than people expect

The memory most people come home with from a well-planned trip is usually something that was not scheduled — a street, a teahouse, a park they walked through because there was time. Leaving a genuine gap in the afternoon is not wasted capacity; it is where the interesting part has room to happen.

There is also a practical version of this argument. Everything is subject to weather, closure, crowding and your own energy on the day, and a plan with slack absorbs those instead of collapsing. Season interacts here too — some months remove whole categories of activity, which is why timing is worth reading before locking the sequence, not after. The notes under season and crowd patterns cover which weeks to aim at.

The version worth building

Region, then three or four cities with a minimum of three nights each, two objectives per day, one afternoon deliberately empty. It will look under-ambitious as a document and it will be correct in practice.
