Mapping Your Home in Five Steps: A Working Feng Shui Practice

Most writing on feng shui starts at the wrong end — discussing cures before anyone has established what is being cured. The practice only becomes usable when it starts from the floor plan you actually have.

Step one: get a plan, not an impression

A hand sketch with measurements is enough, but it has to be drawn rather than imagined. People are reliably wrong about how their own rooms are arranged, and small errors near the centre distort the whole overlay. The five-step walkthrough in how to map your home with the bagua starts from exactly that sketch, and the steps that follow assume you have it.

Step two: the front door decides the orientation

The bagua is aligned to the door you actually use, not to magnetic north and not to the front door the architect drew. This is the step that most often goes wrong, and it invalidates everything downstream of it — the same flat reads completely differently depending on which entrance is treated as the mouth of qi.

Step three: overlay, then look for what is missing

Align the nine sectors over the plan and read it room by room. The informative result is usually structural rather than mystical: a sector that falls outside the building altogether, or one that only exists as a cupboard. Those absences are the reading.

What a real fix looks like

A useful distinction: adjustments that change how a space is used versus additions that merely decorate it.

  • Change of use. A room that nobody enters is genuinely different once it is walked through daily.
  • Change of sightline. A door that opens onto something immediately in front of it is the classic case. There is a worked example in the notes on a front door facing stairs, where the practical remedies are about slowing movement, not about hanging charms.
  • Colour and material. Genuine but downstream. The yearly feng shui colour guidance sits here — it refines a space whose arrangement already works.

If someone proposes an expensive object before discussing how you move through the room, you are being sold something.

The calendar is a separate instrument

Space is one axis; time is the other. Nothing above tells you when to act, which is what the Chinese fortune calendar covers — selecting days for undertakings rather than rearranging furniture. It is a different question and it is worth keeping separate; conflating them is how the practice turns incoherent.

Treat the result as a prompt, not a verdict

The value of going through this properly is that it forces attention onto how you actually use the rooms you live in. Whether that is framed as energy flow or as ordinary spatial honesty, the outcome is that a cupboard nobody opens gets dealt with.
