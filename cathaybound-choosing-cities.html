Choosing Which Chinese Cities to Include, and Which to Leave Out

The standard first-trip mistake is not choosing badly. It is choosing six cities for fourteen days and then discovering that two of those days vanished into station queues and airport transit — leaving eleven days spent mostly in motion.

Count travel days as real days

High-speed rail is genuinely good: up to roughly a thousand kilometres it beats flying door to door once security and airport transfers are counted, and it runs on time. But it is not free — even a four-hour train plus checking out, reaching the station early and finding the hotel at the far end consumes most of a day.

A working rule for a first trip: three nights per city minimum, and assume one city costs you half a day to enter and half to leave. Two weeks realistically supports four cities, five if two of them are close together.

Decide what you actually want each city for

The useful way to choose is by role rather than by reputation, because most of the famous list overlaps heavily:

  • Imperial China, in stone. Xi'an for the terracotta army and the city wall; Beijing for the Forbidden City and the Great Wall in one base.
  • Modern China, at speed. Shanghai, which rewards two nights rather than being used as an arrival hall.
  • Landscape. Guilin for the karst river scenery, Zhangjiajie for the pillar formations — pick one, they are variants on a theme.
  • Slower pace. Chengdu and Hangzhou both work as the place you stop moving; add Suzhou for gardens if you are already near Shanghai.
  • Deep history off the main line. Dunhuang rewards real interest in the Silk Road and a willingness to spend travel time getting there; Pingyao is a simpler version — a walled old town reachable without much effort.

Cluster geographically

What quietly ruins itineraries is region-hopping: Beijing, then Guilin, then Dunhuang looks fine on a map and costs three long travel days. Two sensible shapes for a first trip, both of which avoid it:

  • The eastern corridor. Beijing → Xi'an → Shanghai, possibly with Suzhou or Hangzhou appended. Rail throughout, everything connects, and it covers old China, imperial China and modern China in one pass.
  • One base plus nature. Shanghai → Zhangjiajie or Guilin, with Hangzhou for breathing room. Fewer transitions, more time actually looking at things.

Two weeks, concretely

Four nights Beijing, three Xi'an, four Shanghai with a day trip to Suzhou, three Chengdu — that is fourteen days with one domestic flight at most and every leg on rail. Anything that adds a fifth city is trading two half-days of transit for one afternoon in a place you will not remember.

Once the shape is fixed, worked day-by-day plans save the rest of the effort; start from the itineraries section, which builds them around these clusters rather than around wish lists.

Practical notes that decide things

Book intercity rail a few days ahead during any national holiday period — those windows sell out, and holiday weeks are not interchangeable with normal weeks. Station entry needs more time than a European train: allow forty minutes, more at the large hubs. Everything else — payments, connectivity, getting from airport to centre — is covered in the China travel guides.
