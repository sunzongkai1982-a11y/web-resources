Asking Twice: The Etiquette Rule That Is Actually About Honesty

Anyone who has drawn sticks knows the temptation: the answer was unwelcome, so draw again. The tradition has a firm rule against this, and it is not superstition — the rule is protecting you from a specific and very ordinary failure of judgement.

Re-drawing selects for the answer you wanted

Draw sticks three times about the same question and take the best result, and you have not received guidance. You have run a random generator until it agreed with you, then stopped. The probability that three draws return the happier answer is high, and the practice quietly guarantees you will always be told what you hoped for.

That is what the etiquette forbids. It is the same instinct that makes rules like "one draw per question, per day" worth taking seriously, and the reasoning behind them is set out in the discussion of how often you should draw.

The tradition is more specific than "wait a bit"

The customary framing varies slightly between temples, but the shape is consistent: settle yourself, hold one clear question, and draw once. If it does not resolve, the answer is that the question was not ready — which is itself information. Asking again later, having thought about it properly, is different from immediately re-rolling.

The preparatory side matters more than people expect. Formulating the question is the actual work, and doing it properly takes longer than the draw. Knowing something about who is being asked helps too — the background on Guanyin in iconography and symbol makes the practice considerably less arbitrary.

Reading the stick rather than the verdict

Beginners read the hundred sticks as a ranking — good ones, bad ones — and stop there. In practice the sticks are structured as situations with advice attached, including difficult ones that specify what is being cautioned against. A warning is not a prediction; read as counsel rather than forecast, even the harder results have something actionable in them.

Why this is worth following even if you do not believe

Set the divination aside and the rule still holds: deciding an important question by sampling until you like the result is a bad way to decide things. The etiquette encodes a piece of decision hygiene that predates the vocabulary for it.
