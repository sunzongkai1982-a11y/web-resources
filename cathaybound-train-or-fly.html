Train or Plane? The Distance Rule That Actually Holds

The comparison everyone runs is the wrong one: four hours on a train versus two hours in the air. What matters is door to door — getting to the airport, the ninety-minute buffer nobody admits to, waiting at the far end, then getting into town from an airport that is rarely near anything.

Where rail wins, and by how much

Once you count realistically, high-speed rail wins comfortably up to somewhere around 800–1000 km. Below that the plane's advantage evaporates in security queues and airport transfers, and that is before you price the delay risk.

The clearest demonstration is a short hop. Trains between Hangzhou and Suzhou run at something like twenty-five minutes; nobody sane flies it. The Hangzhou to Suzhou breakdown covers this and the more interesting problem it creates — Suzhou has several stations and picking the wrong one costs longer than the journey did. The whole Yangtze delta works this way, which is why two-day visits there are realistic; see the coverage of Shanghai, Hangzhou and Suzhou.

The overnight sleeper case

At the far end of the distance range something different happens: the sleeper stops competing with flying and starts competing with hotels. A bed you sleep in while covering the distance buys you the next day and saves a night's accommodation.

Harbin to Beijing is the honest example — long enough that the day train eats a full day and short enough that flying feels wasteful. The Harbin–Beijing comparison weighs the options against each other rather than just listing them. Whether this works for you depends almost entirely on whether you can sleep on a train, which is a personal fact worth admitting: if you cannot, the sleeper is expensive accommodation you did not enjoy. Broader northern routes are collected under Beijing, Xi'an and the north.

Where the rule breaks

Two exceptions are common enough to name:

  • Mountain terrain in the southwest. Rail exists but often runs slower than the map suggests, and flying to a regional airport can genuinely save a day.
  • Holiday weeks. During national holidays rail sells out well in advance, and "the train is obviously better" stops being true when the only remaining ticket is standing or nothing. Decision made by availability rather than preference.

Buying tickets, reading timetables and choosing between classes are their own subjects, all handled under trains. Everything else — including what each of these actually costs — is in the China travel guides.

The version worth planning with

Count the whole journey, not the middle of it. That single habit removes most of the confusion about whether China's rail network is fast, which by and large it is.
