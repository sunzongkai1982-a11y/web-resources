Three Versions of the Same Person: Matching the Photo to Where It Lives

People spend a long time getting one headshot right, then use that single image everywhere — on a conference bio, a hospital directory, LinkedIn, and a press kit. The image is good. In two of those four places it is wrong.

The register has to match the surface

A photograph carries information about formality, distance and approachability before anyone registers your face. The same person legitimately needs several registers, and choosing them deliberately is not vanity — it is matching the picture to the room it will be seen in.

How one professional role splits into versions

The clearest way to see this is to look at roles where multiple surfaces genuinely apply.

Executive contexts

Senior roles span an unusually wide range, because the same individual appears in an annual report, on a conference stage and on a product-adjacent page. Those are three different registers:

  • Formal studio. Dark suiting, neutral ground, minimal retouching — built to survive being printed large. See the executive studio notes.
  • Boardroom. Slightly less rigid, often with an implied environment; the register is authority rather than ceremony. See the boardroom notes.
  • Modern / tech. Relaxed clothing, softer background, usually for companies whose culture is the point. See the relaxed executive notes.

Clinical contexts

Healthcare is the opposite problem: the practitioner needs to look competent to peers and approachable to patients, sometimes in the same week, and those pull in different directions.

  • Natural. Warm and unhurried — the register for patient-facing pages. See the nurse headshots notes.
  • Professional network. Straighter, more neutral, for recruiters and hiring managers: the nurse headshots guidance.
  • Graduation / qualification. Cleaner and plainer, because it is a record rather than a pitch: graduation nurse headshots.

What must not change between versions

Three things hold the set together so it reads as one person rather than three strangers:

  1. The same apparent age. Nothing damages credibility faster than two versions that look years apart.
  2. Consistent crop and eye line. So the images sit together on a page.
  3. Consistent framing of the face. The versioning should be in clothing, background and expression — not in how much of your head is visible.

Breaking those is how "several versions" turns into "several people", which is worse than having one photo.

Keeping the set usable

Export one full-resolution master per register and keep them together; a set generated only as square crops will need regenerating the first time someone asks for a wide banner alongside a bio. Generating several registers from the same source material is how the identity stays consistent — that is the approach at Portraze.
