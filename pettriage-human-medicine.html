Human Medicines in the House: Why One Pill Is Not "Just One Pill"

If your animal may have swallowed a human medicine, call a vet or an animal poison helpline now. This page explains how the risk is worked out — it is not a substitute for that call, and waiting to see whether symptoms appear is how mild cases become serious ones.

The question owners ask is "is this poisonous?" The question that actually decides the outcome is a different one: how much of it, per kilogram of animal, and does this species clear it at all?

Why cats and dogs are not interchangeable

Species differ in the enzymes that clear drugs. A dog has some capacity to process paracetamol; a cat essentially does not — there is no safe dose of paracetamol for a cat, and a single tablet can be fatal. Conversely, some compounds that are routine for cats are dangerous for dogs. "My friend's dog ate one and was fine" carries no information whatsoever about your animal.

This is also why size matters more than anything else: the same 500 mg tablet represents a completely different dose to a 4 kg cat than to a 34 kg labrador. Dose per kilogram is the number the clinic will calculate first.

The usual household suspects

Most accidental poisonings involve medicines sitting in a bag, a bedside drawer, or dropped on the floor. Among the ones seen repeatedly:

  • Painkillers. Paracetamol and ibuprofen are the classic pair — the the acetaminophen toxicity calculator for dogs works the dose through by weight.
  • Antidepressants. Often surprisingly appealing to dogs, and slow to act. Notes in antidepressant poisoning in dogs.
  • ADHD medication. Stimulant formulations are potent at small doses; see ADHD medication poisoning.
  • Antihistamines. Used at home by owners and often assumed harmless — see diphenhydramine notes for dogs and cats.
  • Supplements. The category people forget entirely. Herbal products are not automatically benign, and timelines differ: the 5-HTP supplement poisoning write-up covers both species, with the symptom timeline shown hour by hour.
  • Antifreeze. Not a medicine, but it lives next to them in the garage and is sweet-tasting — there is a separate toxicity calculator for it.

The four facts a vet will ask for

Having these ready before you dial saves the minutes that matter:

  1. The exact product name and strength in milligrams per unit.
  2. How many units are missing — count the remaining tablets in the pack rather than estimating.
  3. Your animal's current weight.
  4. How long ago, and whether they were seen eating anything else.

Bring the packaging with you. The box resolves every ambiguity about strength and inactive ingredients, and for combination products the second active is often the one that causes trouble.

Things that do not help

Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to — with corrosives, sharp objects or an already drowsy animal it does measurable harm. Do not wait for symptoms: for a number of these compounds the window in which treatment works best is measured in hours, and animals hide early signs well.
