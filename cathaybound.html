China Travel Planning: The Logistics People Forget Until They Arrive

Trip planning for China has a particular failure mode. People research attractions in detail and then arrive unable to pay for a bottle of water. None of this is hard, but almost none of it is improvable on arrival.

Payments first, everything else second

This is the one that catches people. Card acceptance for foreign-issued cards is inconsistent, and the default domestic method is a QR-code wallet tied to a local bank account. Sort out mobile payment before you fly, not on your first morning. Carry some cash regardless — slower, but it always works.

Eating, and who pays when

Restaurants range from picture menus and QR ordering to fully verbal. There are three things that surprise first-time visitors: in cheaper places you often pay before eating, the bill arrives as a QR code rather than a folder, and nobody brings you a receipt unless you ask. The guide to ordering food covers how this actually goes.

Between cities

High-speed rail is usually the right answer up to roughly a thousand kilometres — faster door to door than flying once security and airport transit are counted, and far more predictable. What people underestimate is station entry: allow more time than you would for a European train. The three busiest corridors each get their own notes — Beijing to Shanghai, Beijing to Xi'an and Shanghai to Hangzhou.

When something goes wrong

Losing a passport is stressful mainly because nobody knows the sequence or the order. For China specifically there is a documented process for a lost passport — police report, consulate, replacement travel document — in the sequence you will need it.

Choosing where to go

With limited time, it is better to do fewer cities properly. The city guides cover onward logistics rather than just sights, and there are worked day-by-day plans including three days in Beijing and two days in Shanghai, written to actually be walked. The routes section has the full list of intercity options.

Before departure

Assume services you rely on at home are unavailable, and arrange replacements before you land rather than after. The guide index is organised by problem rather than by destination, which is closer to how a trip goes wrong. Everything at CathayBound is written from real timetables and prices, checked on the ground.
