The Basking Spot Is Not the Temperature

Ask most new keepers what temperature they run and you will get a single figure, quoted with some confidence. It is nearly always the basking spot. And it is nearly always the least useful number in the whole setup.

One number cannot describe a gradient

A heat lamp does not warm an enclosure evenly. It makes a hot point directly beneath it and a falloff in every direction away from that point. The animal does not sit at the hot point all day — it moves across the falloff, warming up at the top and shedding heat at the margins. The thing it actually needs is a usable range, not a peak.

This is why two enclosures can report the same basking temperature and house animals in completely different condition. One has a genuine spread from warm side to cool side; the other is a box where the "cool end" is barely cooler than the hot end, so the animal has nowhere to go. Reading the temperature and humidity targets as a range rather than a number changes what you buy and where you put it. It is also why the temperature and humidity tool asks about both ends of the enclosure instead of asking for one reading.

Escaping the heat is the half everyone skips

Setup advice spends most of its words on producing heat and very few on the ability to leave it. Both matter, and the second one is where most failures sit. An animal that cannot cool down does not have a heating setup — it has a hot box with a lamp in it. A hide on the cool side, substrate that holds some moisture, and enough horizontal room for a real falloff do more for a lizard than another twenty watts overhead.

The species-level pages treat this as one problem rather than two. The bearded dragon care sheet is a good example: basking and cooling are described together, because designing them separately is what produces the hot box.

Then there is night

The other half of the mistake is assuming the daytime target holds around the clock. Most captive species come from places that cool off after dark, and a setup that holds daytime temperatures straight through the night removes a signal the animal uses. Whether a night drop is needed, and how much, is species-specific — which is exactly why it belongs on a species page rather than in a general rule of thumb.

What to actually measure

Two probes, not one: one at the basking point, one at the far cool end, both at the height the animal actually lives at rather than on the glass. If those two readings are close together, the enclosure is too small or the lamp is too broadly heating — and no amount of adjusting the peak will fix it.
