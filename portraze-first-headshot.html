Your First Professional Photo Is Usually Wrong in One of Two Ways

Ask someone for their first professional photo and you generally get one of two things: a cropped picture from a night out, or someone wearing a suit borrowed for the occasion, staring like a passport applicant. Both fail for the same reason — neither looks like the person who would turn up on the Monday.

Mistake the first: borrowed authority

Students and new graduates tend to dress up past their field. It comes from a reasonable instinct — this is a formal occasion so dress formally — but it produces an image that looks like someone auditioning, and recruiters read it that way.

The fix is not dressing down, it is dressing accurately. Whatever people in the job you want actually wear is the answer, and for most graduate roles that is considerably plainer than expected. The notes for students and graduation photos skew deliberately simple for this reason.

Mistake the second: no standard at all

The opposite failure is treating it as casual — adequate lighting, a decent phone, done. The trouble is this image becomes the baseline for years, appearing on applications where the comparison set is everyone else's serious attempt.

This is worth understanding early rather than correcting at thirty, because the correction is retroactive: you replace old photos piecemeal and end up with three versions from different periods floating around, which is its own problem.

Academic and industry want different things

Faculty pages and academic profiles reward the plainest possible treatment — the concern is being taken seriously in a context where showiness is negatively correlated with credibility. The professor headshots guidance reflects that plainly.

Industry early-career roles want competent and approachable rather than either extreme. Two concrete examples where the register is fairly specific: data scientists, where company pages favour something plain and credible, and product managers, whose team pages sit slightly warmer because the role is inherently about talking to people.

The one thing to get right

Resolution. Ask for full-resolution masters now, at the point it costs nothing, and store them where you will still find them in five years. Everything gets easier from there — every future surface is a crop of something you already own rather than another attempt to match a photo you have outgrown.
