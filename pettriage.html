Pet Poisoning Triage: What Decides the Outcome in the First Ten Minutes

If your animal may have eaten something toxic, phone a vet or an animal poison helpline now. This page is for understanding what happens next, not for deciding whether to call.

Owners usually panic about the wrong variable. "Is chocolate poisonous to dogs?" is the wrong question. The right one is: how much, of what kind, per kilogram of animal?

Dose per kilogram is the whole game

A single grape is not the same event as a bowl of raisins, and a square of baking chocolate is not the same event for a Labrador as for a Chihuahua. This is why triage tools ask for weight before they say anything about risk — without it, every answer is a guess.

The five things you will be asked

Have these ready before you dial:

  1. The exact product, not the category. "Chocolate" is useless; dark 85% baking chocolate versus milk chocolate is a tenfold difference in theobromine.
  2. Roughly how much is missing from the packet.
  3. How long ago it happened.
  4. The animal's current weight — and estimate honestly, because small errors change the answer.
  5. Species. Cats metabolise several common substances far worse than dogs.

PetTriage turns those five inputs into a risk level and a set of next steps, which matters most in the gap between noticing something and getting through to someone.

The usual household suspects

  • Chocolate and cocoa — risk tracks with cocoa solids and body weight; see the pages on chocolate spreads and products.
  • Grapes and raisins — no established safe dose exists, which is why both grapes and raisins are treated seriously rather than watched.
  • Xylitol — in sugar-free gum and many "no sugar added" products; dangerous at very small doses in dogs (the xylitol in sugar-free gum).
  • Alliums — green onion and garlic powder damage red blood cells cumulatively, so the danger is easy to miss.
  • Human painkillers — ibuprofen and aspirin are not equivalent in dogs, and a human dose can be catastrophic.
  • Alcohol and macadamia — see alcohol and macadamia nuts.

Three things not to do

  • Do not induce vomiting unless a vet tells you to. With corrosives, sharp objects, or an already drowsy animal it does more harm than good.
  • Do not wait for symptoms. Several of these have a lag of hours to days, and the useful treatment window is earlier than that.
  • Do not dose from internet tables alone. Product concentrations vary enormously.

Who to call

In the US, the ASPCA Animal Poison Control Center and the Pet Poison Helpline both run 24-hour lines staffed by veterinarians. If you are already driving to a clinic, phone first so they can prepare rather than arriving unannounced.
